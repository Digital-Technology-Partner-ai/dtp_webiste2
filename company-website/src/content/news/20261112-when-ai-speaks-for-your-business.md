An AI agent carrying your company's name could answer a prospective customer's question before anyone on your team sees it. Who decides what it is allowed to promise?

That is the business question behind ChatGPT's new Business Agents. The attraction is easy to understand. A customer can ask a question, compare options and follow up without leaving the conversation. For the company, it creates another place where the quality of its answers matters.

## What OpenAI is introducing

The Verge's report of 10 November describes a beta initially available to eligible e-commerce and online service businesses in the US. When a participating business appears in a ChatGPT response, a customer can start a conversation with its Business Agent.

Businesses supply instructions and business context. OpenAI says these conversations will be clearly labelled and separate from the customer's main ChatGPT conversation. A Business Hub lets companies claim and verify their organisation, and see insights including mentions in ChatGPT responses and the topics in which their business appears.

That initial US scope matters. This is a development for UK leaders to watch, not a reason to assume their business can launch one today.

## A verified identity is only the start

Knowing that an agent represents the real business is useful. The business still needs to decide which answers it can stand behind.

Consider a customer asking whether installation is included. A product page says it is. A service document lists exclusions. A recent pricing change has not reached either document. A salesperson would need to resolve that conflict before making a commitment. An agent needs an approved way to handle it too.

Uploading both documents does not settle which one takes precedence. Nor does an instruction to be helpful decide when the answer should be, "I need someone to check that for you."

The preparation is partly a knowledge-management job. Current prices, terms, exclusions and service boundaries need an owner. Conflicting information needs resolving. Changes need to reach the material the agent uses.

## Test the awkward questions

A demonstration built around straightforward product questions can miss the cases that matter most to customer trust.

Before opening a customer-facing agent, work through questions such as:

- Which approved source governs prices, availability and exclusions, and who updates it?
- What happens when a customer asks for a discount, guarantee or exception the agent cannot authorise?
- How does it respond when the information is missing, contradictory or out of date?
- Can the customer reach a person without starting the explanation again?
- Who reviews wrong or misleading answers, corrects the underlying information and checks the fix?

These are proposed operating checks, not a description of safeguards already proven in OpenAI's beta. The reported launch does not establish how an individual company's agent will perform against them.

Start with one customer journey. Use ordinary questions, then add ambiguity, unusual circumstances and requests outside the agreed limits. Judge whether the answers are accurate and useful, and whether the agent stops when it should. A fluent answer is not enough.

## Why this matters in practice

The opportunity is to make it easier for customers to understand what a business offers. That depends on the company doing the less glamorous work behind the conversation.

The approved knowledge should remain something the business owns and maintains, rather than a collection of instructions nobody can explain after the first setup. Keep a record of what the agent may say, what it must refer to a person and who is responsible when the information changes.
